About this map

How the data were collected, what the AI did, and what to keep in mind.

How it was made

  1. A starting list of people. We started from a hand-made list of Dutch metascientists and members of Dutch open-science networks, kept the academics (PhD candidates, postdocs, researchers, professors), and found their ORCID and OpenAlex records; when OpenAlex split one of them over several author profiles, the profiles were merged. Most people on the site were found later, as authors of the metascience papers; the site no longer distinguishes between the two.
  2. Their papers came from OpenAlex (all papers on their author profiles) and Crossref (papers where the publisher recorded their ORCID). Only scientific papers were kept: journal articles, reviews, preprints with a DOI, books, chapters, conference papers, editorials and letters; not blog posts, OSF project pages, datasets or peer reviews.
  3. A search of OpenAlex added every paper since 2005 with an author at a Dutch institution whose title or abstract uses metascience terms (such as replicability, preregistration, publication bias, research integrity, Bayes factor, scientometrics, research assessment).
  4. An AI judged every paper (about 50,000 titles and abstracts): is it metascience, and if so, which of eight themes? Papers it called metascience were then checked a second time with stricter instructions, and every remaining paper was then checked once more by reading it (see below); about 6,300 remain. The model was gpt-oss:20b, run in Ollama on a GPU of SURF Research Cloud, with written instructions that say what counts (for example: a replication of a published finding, a study of peer review, a method for better statistical inference) and what does not (a study that merely shares its data, a meta-analysis of a drug, the reproducibility of a lab measurement, DNA replication).
  5. The map of themes: each metascience paper's title and abstract were turned into a numeric fingerprint with the bge-m3 model, placed on a 2-D map with UMAP, and grouped into 20 clusters (k-means); the AI named each cluster from the 20 titles nearest its centre. Several of those names fitted those 20 titles but not the whole cluster (for example, a cluster on hypothesis testing in general was called "Bayesian"), so all 20 names were revised by Claude (Anthropic) after reading each cluster's most distinctive title words, its most common keywords and topics, its most cited titles and a random sample of titles. One cluster has no single subject and is called "Mixed metascience topics". The names and the reasons are in scripts/cluster_names.json.
  6. Who is on the site: everyone with a Dutch institution on at least one of these metascience papers, plus the people on the starting list. Co-authorship lines use only papers with at most 30 authors.
  7. History: the reference lists of all papers came from OpenAlex. Landmark papers per theme are works that many of the theme's papers cite and that the whole of science cites often for their field and year (see the History page for the rules); at most 20 per theme. Next to them, each theme shows its 5 most cited papers by Dutch metascientists (papers on this site, by total citations). Blog debates come from the Open Science Blogs Archive: a script found moments when several different bloggers wrote about the same thing at the same time, and Claude read those posts, rejected chance word bursts, and wrote each title and one-sentence summary; a second search of the whole archive for each debate's names found more posts, which Claude checked. As a rule, a debate is shown when at least 5 different bloggers wrote about it, and it links to posts by at least 3 bloggers. Historical events were collected from Wikipedia by Claude agents; each event keeps the article's own words for its date, and a script kept only events whose words and year it found in the article.

How good are the judgements?

Two automatic checks. The language model first flagged 10,598 papers as metascience; a spot check showed that about one in six of these were not (for example data infrastructure for one field, or ordinary systematic reviews of a medical question), so a second, stricter check removed 2,301 of them. A random sample showed that this second check was still not good enough, and that it also removed about one in five papers that were metascience after all (mostly bibliometric and general statistics papers; these are missing).

A check of every paper by reading it. All papers that remained and have a Dutch author were then judged one by one from title and abstract, following written rules (scripts/hand_check_rules.md). This was done by Claude, an AI assistant, working as 14 separate reviewers, and not by human experts. To measure its quality, each reviewer also judged 15 papers whose answer had been decided beforehand (agreement 93%; it never called a paper "not metascience" when the decided answer was "metascience"), and 100 papers were judged by two reviewers (same verdict for 92; never "metascience" against "not metascience"). A sample of 40 removals was checked again: 39 were right. In total 571 papers were removed; the list, with a reason for each, is scripts/excluded_by_hand.csv.

What remains. Papers judged "unclear" (about one in eight, for example a replication of a genetic association, or a critique of one specific meta-analysis) stay on the site. From the checks above, we expect that roughly 1 to 2% of the papers shown are clearly not metascience. The theme of each paper and its explanation were written by the language model and were not checked by hand.

Known limitations

  • Author identities come from OpenAlex, which sometimes splits one person over several profiles or merges two people with the same name. A person can therefore appear twice, or have someone else's paper.
  • "Dutch" means: an institution in the Netherlands on that paper. People who moved abroad still appear with their Dutch papers; Dutch researchers working abroad do not. Each person is shown with one institution: the Dutch institution on most of their papers. OpenAlex sometimes gives an author the institution of a co-author, so a second institution was often wrong.
  • Everyone with a metascience paper is on the site. Writing one paper on a metascience topic does not make someone a metascientist; the number of papers shows how much of a person's work is metascience.
  • Missing abstracts: for some of the metascience papers OpenAlex has no abstract, so the AI saw only the title, and term counts are lower bounds.
  • Coverage: papers not in OpenAlex, and papers before 2005 without an author from the starting list (whose full publication lists were collected), are missing.

Privacy

The site shows only public bibliographic information (names as they appear on papers, ORCID identifiers, institutions on papers, titles, years, DOIs, co-authorships, citation counts) and the AI judgements. It contains no email addresses, job titles or memberships. If you want to be removed or corrected, open an issue in the GitHub repository.

The personal view ("Find yourself" on the overview page) only highlights public data that is already on the site. The chosen person is kept in your browser and in the page address (?me=), so a link can be shared; nothing is sent to a server, because the site has none. Anyone can choose any name.
