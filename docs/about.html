About this map

How the data were collected, what the AI did, and what to keep in mind.

How it was made

  1. A starting list of people. We started from a hand-made list of Dutch metascientists and members of Dutch open-science networks, kept the academics (PhD candidates, postdocs, researchers, professors), and found their ORCID and OpenAlex records; when OpenAlex split one of them over several author profiles, the profiles were merged. Most people on the site were found later, as authors of the metascience papers; the site no longer distinguishes between the two.
  2. Their papers came from OpenAlex (all papers on their author profiles) and Crossref (papers where the publisher recorded their ORCID). Only scientific papers were kept: journal articles, reviews, preprints with a DOI, books, chapters, conference papers, editorials and letters; not blog posts, OSF project pages, deposits on Zenodo or Figshare (such as project deliverables and slides), datasets or peer reviews. The same rule applies to the papers found by the search below (some items left out).
  3. A search of OpenAlex added every paper since 2005 with an author at a Dutch institution whose title or abstract uses metascience terms (such as replicability, preregistration, publication bias, research integrity, Bayes factor, scientometrics, research assessment). In November 2026 terms on research funding and grant review, reporting standards, research data and software, preprints and scholarly publishing, research careers and research evaluation were added (the full list is in scripts/terms.py).
  4. More papers by frequent authors. For people who are not on the starting list, the search finds only papers that use one of its terms. For everyone not on the starting list with at least two metascience papers on the site, their other papers with an author at a Dutch institution were added when OpenAlex gives them one of 238 keywords. The keywords were chosen from the data: each is on at least 10 papers on the site, and in the full publication lists of the starting list at least 60% of the papers with that keyword were judged metascience (the list is in scripts/author_lists_input.json). This was done once; people who reached two papers only through these added papers were not looked up again.
  5. An AI judged every paper (about 63,000 titles and abstracts): is it metascience, and if so, which of eight themes? Papers it called metascience were then checked a second time with stricter instructions, and every remaining paper was then checked once more by reading it (see below); about 6,300 remain. The model was gpt-oss:20b, run in Ollama on a GPU of SURF Research Cloud, with written instructions that say what counts (for example: a replication of a published finding, a study of peer review, a method for better statistical inference) and what does not (a study that merely shares its data, a meta-analysis of a drug, the reproducibility of a lab measurement, DNA replication).
  6. The map of themes: each metascience paper's title and abstract were turned into a numeric fingerprint with the bge-m3 model, placed on a 2-D map with UMAP, and grouped into 20 clusters (k-means); the AI named each cluster from the 20 titles nearest its centre. Several of those names fitted those 20 titles but not the whole cluster (for example, a cluster on hypothesis testing in general was called "Bayesian"), so all 20 names were revised by Claude (Anthropic) after reading each cluster's most distinctive title words, its most common keywords and topics, its most cited titles and a random sample of titles. One cluster has no single subject and is called "Mixed metascience topics". The names and the reasons are in scripts/cluster_names.json. Papers added later (November 2026) joined the cluster whose centre is nearest to their fingerprint, and the map was computed again starting from the earlier positions, so the clusters and their names stayed the same.
  7. Which papers and people are on the site: metascience papers with an author at a Dutch institution, and everyone at a Dutch institution on at least one of them. Papers by people on the starting list are shown only when they have a Dutch institution, or when OpenAlex has no institution for that person on that paper (mostly Dutch preprints and theses); some papers from before or after their time in the Netherlands were left out. Some publishers give all authors one shared (or nearly the same) address text, such as "From University of Oxford … and University Medical Center Utrecht", and OpenAlex then gives every author all those institutions; such a paper alone does not make anyone Dutch. A paper that appears more than once (a preprint and its journal version, or a statement published in several journals) is shown once: as the journal version, with that version's year and citations (some copies left out; copies are recognised by the same title and a shared author, so copies whose titles differ slightly remain). Co-authorship lines use only papers with at most 30 authors.
  8. History: the reference lists of all papers came from OpenAlex. Landmark papers per theme are works that many of the theme's papers cite and that the whole of science cites often for their field and year (see the History page for the rules); at most 20 per theme. Next to them, each theme shows its 5 most cited papers by Dutch metascientists (papers on this site, by total citations). Blog debates come from the Open Science Blogs Archive: a script found moments when several different bloggers wrote about the same thing at the same time, and Claude read those posts, rejected chance word bursts, and wrote each title and one-sentence summary; a second search of the whole archive for each debate's names found more posts, which Claude checked. As a rule, a debate is shown when at least 5 different bloggers wrote about it, and it links to posts by at least 3 bloggers. Historical events were collected from Wikipedia by Claude agents; each event keeps the article's own words for its date, and a script kept only events whose words and year it found in the article.

How good are the judgements?

Two automatic checks. The language model first flagged 10,598 papers as metascience; a spot check showed that about one in six of these were not (for example data infrastructure for one field, or ordinary systematic reviews of a medical question), so a second, stricter check removed 2,301 of them. A random sample showed that this second check was still not good enough, and that it also removed about one in five papers that were metascience after all (mostly bibliometric and general statistics papers; these are missing).

A check of every paper by reading it. All papers that remained and have a Dutch author were then judged one by one from title and abstract, following written rules (scripts/hand_check_rules.md). This was done by Claude, an AI assistant, working as 16 separate reviewers, and not by human experts; papers that came onto the site in later rebuilds were checked in the same way. To measure its quality, each reviewer also judged 15 papers whose answer had been decided beforehand (agreement 94%; it never called a paper "not metascience" when the decided answer was "metascience"), and 115 papers were judged by two reviewers (same verdict for 106; never "metascience" against "not metascience"). A sample of 40 removals was checked again: 39 were right. The papers added in November 2026 (about 3,500) were checked in the same way by 9 more reviewers. Their agreement with the decided answers was lower (81%), but almost all differences were "unclear" where the decided answer was "not metascience", so such papers stay on the site; again no reviewer called a paper "not metascience" when the decided answer was "metascience", and of 135 papers judged by two reviewers 127 got the same verdict (never "metascience" against "not metascience").

Revised rules for borderline papers. After these rounds, about one in six papers on the site had been judged "unclear". The rules were then made explicit for the borderline cases: research practice within one field counts (for example research priorities, evidence standards or research data infrastructure for one field, and methods for developing clinical guidelines), and so does a comment on one study that makes a general point about research practice; a debate about what one study shows does not, nor do quality appraisals of specific clinical guidelines, retraction notices, or ordinary studies that include a replication sample. All "unclear" papers and all removed papers (about 2,200) were judged again under these rules by 6 more reviewers (of 120 papers judged by two of them, 113 got the same verdict); groups of papers that reviewers had decided differently were then settled by one decision per group. "Unclear" is now used only when title and abstract do not tell enough. In total 571 papers were removed; the list, with a reason for each, is scripts/excluded_by_hand.csv.

What remains. Papers still judged "unclear" (about 60, mostly papers without an abstract) stay on the site. From the checks above, we expect that roughly 1 to 2% of the papers shown are clearly not metascience. The theme of each paper and its explanation were written by the language model and were not checked by hand.

Known limitations

  • Author identities come from OpenAlex, which sometimes splits one person over several profiles or merges two people with the same name. Profiles with the same ORCID are shown as one person. A person without an ORCID on some of their papers can still appear twice, or have someone else's paper.
  • "Dutch" means: an institution in the Netherlands on that paper. People who moved abroad still appear with their Dutch papers; Dutch researchers working abroad do not. Each person is shown with one institution: the Dutch institution on most of their papers. OpenAlex sometimes gives an author the institution of a co-author, so a second institution was often wrong. When two institutions are on equally many papers, a medical centre wins over the university in the same city (addresses such as "University Medical Center Groningen, University of Groningen" name both); otherwise the institution on the most recent paper is shown.
  • Everyone with a metascience paper is on the site. Writing one paper on a metascience topic does not make someone a metascientist; the number of papers shows how much of a person's work is metascience.
  • Missing abstracts: for some of the metascience papers OpenAlex has no abstract, so the AI saw only the title, and term counts are lower bounds.
  • Coverage: papers not in OpenAlex are missing. For people who are not on the starting list (whose full publication lists were collected), papers are missing when they use none of the search terms and carry none of the keywords above, and papers before 2005 are found only through the keywords (so only for people with at least two metascience papers). A person who is Dutch only through a shared address text (see above) is missing too.

Privacy

The site shows only public bibliographic information (names as they appear on papers, ORCID identifiers, institutions on papers, titles, years, DOIs, co-authorships, citation counts) and the AI judgements. It contains no email addresses, job titles or memberships. If you want to be removed or corrected, open an issue in the GitHub repository.

The personal view ("Find yourself" on the overview page) only highlights public data that is already on the site. The chosen person is kept in your browser and in the page address (?me=), so a link can be shared; nothing is sent to a server, because the site has none. Anyone can choose any name.

The Socials page uses public posts on Bluesky and Mastodon (see below). It names only people who post regularly about metascience, and quotes a few of their public posts with a link to the original. If an account was matched to you by mistake, or you do not want to be included, open an issue in the GitHub repository and you will be removed.

How the Socials page was made

The Socials page is built only from public data: public profiles and public posts on Bluesky and Mastodon, read through the public interfaces that both platforms offer to programs, without logging in.

Finding accounts. For every person on this site we looked for accounts in three ways.

  1. Accounts that people listed themselves, in their ORCID record or in Wikidata.
  2. A search by first and last name on Bluesky and on three Mastodon servers (mastodon.social, fediscience.org and akademienl.social), keeping accounts whose display name contains both names. Such an account was accepted when it was the only match on that platform and its profile names the person's ORCID, their institution or its city, or when it is on a Mastodon server for scientists.
  3. The other name matches were judged by AI reviewers (Claude), who compared the profile text and the five most recent posts with the person's institution and paper titles, and answered "yes" only when the account clearly belongs to the person.

Accounts that never posted or no longer exist were left out. Automatic copies of an account on the other platform (by the Bridgy Fed bridge) were replaced by the original account, and accounts that moved were followed to their new address. One account is never given to two different people with the same name.

Only people who work in the Netherlands now. Many people on this site worked in the Netherlands for some years and then moved abroad. The Socials page counts only people who work in the Netherlands now (November 2026). AI reviewers (Claude) judged this for every person from the current profile texts, recent posts and the addresses on their papers. When neither showed where someone works and their last paper with a Dutch address was older than 2023, the person was left out unless the site owner knew otherwise. This check also removed a few accounts that turned out to belong to someone else with the same name.

Reading the posts. All public posts of these accounts were downloaded in November 2026. Only a person's own posts and replies are counted, not reposts of other people's posts. A post counts as a metascience post when it contains one of 38 metascience terms, grouped in five themes. The terms are based on those used to find the papers on this site, with a few short forms used on social media (such as "prereg" and "OSF"). Capitals, hyphens and the "#" of hashtags are ignored. Plain "replication" is not a term, because it mostly means DNA replication.

Who is named. People are named on the Socials page only when they post regularly about metascience: at least 3 of their own posts contain a metascience term, and these are at least 1% of all their own posts. "Peer review" and "impact factor" do not count for this, because almost every academic uses them. Everyone else is only counted in the totals.

Limitations. Only accounts we could find are included, so people who post under another name, or on other Mastodon servers that we could not search, are missing. Some name matches may still be wrong. A term in a post does not always mean the post is about metascience (a job advertisement can mention open science). Interactions are counted from mentions, reposts and replies; on Bluesky, replies are known for metascience posts only. Likes and reposts were counted at the time of download.
