# Is this paper metascience? Rules for the hand check

Metascience (meta-research, research on research) is research whose OBJECT is research itself: how scientists
do, design, analyse, report, check, share, publish, review, fund, evaluate, reward or communicate research, or how
to draw better inferences from research data in general.

The test: is the object of the paper research itself, or is it a substantive question, a technology, a
measurement, a disease, a domain? A paper that merely USES good practices is not metascience.

## Answer YES

- **Replication**: a replication of a published finding in any field (psychology, economics, software
  engineering, a "failed direct replication", "replication and extension", multi-lab tests of an effect); studies
  of whether published findings replicate (including candidate-gene findings); reproducibility of published
  results, analyses, meta-analyses or code; the replication crisis.
- **Research practices and bias**: questionable research practices, p-hacking, publication bias, selective
  reporting, spin, reporting quality of published research, reporting guidelines and checklists (also for one
  field, e.g. CONSORT or Utstein extensions), core outcome sets for trials, preregistration, registered reports,
  research waste, statistical power or sample size practice in published research, how often studies are
  registered, discrepancies between registrations and publications.
- **Methods of evidence synthesis in general**: how to do systematic reviews or meta-analyses well, their
  registration, reproducibility, automation (screening tools), GRADE and similar frameworks.
- **Open science**: data sharing, open data, open materials, open code, open access and preprints as practices of
  researchers, research data management, FAIR principles applied to research in general, open science policy,
  education or interventions for open science.
- **Peer review and publishing**: studies of peer review, journals, editors, editorial practice, retractions,
  predatory journals, authorship, instructions to authors, conflicts of interest disclosure.
- **Research integrity and misconduct**: fraud, misconduct, responsible conduct of research, research climate,
  integrity training, trustworthiness checks of published studies.
- **Statistical methods for inference in general**: significance testing, p values, Bayes factors, equivalence
  tests, effect sizes, power analysis, multiple comparisons, meta-analytic methods, publication-bias methods,
  multiverse / many-analysts approaches, prior elicitation, critiques of common statistical misconceptions.
- **Scientometrics and research evaluation**: bibliometrics, citation analysis, altmetrics, journal indicators,
  publication trends, productivity of researchers or labs, collaboration patterns, research assessment, rankings
  of universities — also when it is about one field or one country (a bibliometric study OF a medical
  subspecialty is still a bibliometric study).
- **Science policy and research ethics**: research funding, academic careers, research culture, science-policy
  interface, research ethics (informed consent in research, ethics review, ethics of trial designs such as
  human challenge trials), science communication and science journalism as a topic of study, trust in science.

A paper does NOT have to be empirical: position papers, commentaries, editorials, guidelines and reports ABOUT
research practice count.

## Answer NO

- A substantive study, even a good one: effects of treatments, risk factors, biomarkers, genetics, prognosis,
  diagnosis, psychology or social science findings that are not replications.
- Systematic reviews, scoping reviews and meta-analyses that answer a substantive question (effects,
  cost-effectiveness, accuracy of tests or prediction models, prevalence, how a construct is measured, which
  interventions exist), even when they assess risk of bias as part of their method.
- Reliability, validity, measurement properties or fairness of tests, instruments, classification systems,
  imaging or lab measurements in one field; measurement methods for one field (accelerometry, EMA reliability).
- Methods or confounders for one specific field or application (e.g. "minimizing microbiome confounders in
  cardiovascular research", a statistical method for one clinical problem).
- Data infrastructure, data standards, databases, ontologies, software, corpora or tools for one domain or one
  kind of data, even when they are called "open", "FAIR" or "reproducible"; studies that merely USE shared data.
- Open data / linked data of governments, companies or utilities; privacy and data protection outside research.
- Clinical practice, clinical guidelines for a disease, quality of care, evidence-based medicine in practice,
  drug regulation and drug safety, health economics.
- Research agendas or priority setting for one disease or topic; descriptions or deliverables of a consortium,
  network or project for one domain; editorials introducing a substantive special issue.
- Ethics, governance or trust concerning a technology (AI, biotechnology, dual use in industry) or a profession,
  not concerning research.
- Education, management, business or economics topics that are not about research.

## Answer UNCLEAR

Use "unclear" when the paper is genuinely borderline or when title and abstract do not tell enough (e.g. no
abstract and an ambiguous title), for example: a critique of one specific substantive meta-analysis; research
methodology for one field that discusses reproducibility of that field; a genetic replication cohort;
reproducibility of brain imaging results; participatory research approaches; a journal's yearly editorial.
Unclear papers stay on the website, so only use "no" when you are confident.
